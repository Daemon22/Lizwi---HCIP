Sensory Layer — Camera / Recognition Zone

too far for control — move closer
The system looks for a person first (face presence), then whether they're oriented toward the camera (engaged), and only then arms the hand-gesture zone on the right side of frame. Microphone stays muted by default — turning it on signals "active conversation" and pauses gesture control, so a chat with someone next to you won't be read as scroll commands. "Hand: motion-zone" is a lightweight stand-in gesture sensor with no external dependencies. Tapping it tries to load a real 21-point hand-skeleton model instead — that needs to fetch model files from a CDN, so it only works when this file is opened directly in your own browser (not inside this chat's sandboxed preview), and it falls back automatically if it can't load. "Posture" adds a body sensor — shoulder lean, which arm is raised, whether you're turned toward or away — plain geometry, same CDN caveat as hand tracking. This deliberately does not attempt to read emotion from your face: that's not reliable science, and for a tool meant to speak for you, a wrong guess about how you feel is worse than no guess. If you want the system to communicate a feeling, teach it as a sign below — "frustrated," "tired," whatever you want said — so it's always your word for it, not the software's.

Understanding Layer — Conversational Engagement

This is for whatever's on the other end of a conversation — a person, or an AI — deciding when it's actually their turn to respond. A fixed "wait N seconds of silence" timer is a bad model for anyone signing or gesturing: a real pause while searching for the next sign looks identical to being finished. This instead watches whether the hands are still active, and treats stillness right after a burst of motion as "probably still thinking," not "done" — the grace period adapts instead of being one fixed number for everyone.

Communication Layer — Sign Trainer & Transcriber

This doesn't know any sign language out of the box — nobody could responsibly claim that from a browser tab. What it does is learn signs you teach it: you show it a sign a few times under a label, it remembers the shape and motion of your hand, and from then on it recognizes that sign live and adds it to a running transcript. That makes it a personal sign-to-text tool that grows with you, not a general ASL/BSL translator — the vocabulary is exactly what you put in. Requires real hand-skeleton tracking (the "hand" button above) to be active first, since this needs actual finger positions, not just motion. Labels aren't limited to words for objects or actions — you can just as well train a sign for "frustrated," "tired," or "I need a break." The system will never guess how you feel from your face; this is how you tell it, on your terms, in your own words. The "standard vocabulary" checkbox tags a sign as one meant to be the same for everyone — a real shared sign language recognizer needs proper training on a real dataset from many signers, which is a separate, much bigger project than this app. What this checkbox does honestly is keep your exports organized by intent from day one — personal shorthand kept separate from anything meant to become standardized later — rather than pretend that distinction doesn't matter until it's too late to untangle.
